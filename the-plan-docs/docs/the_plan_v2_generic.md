# The Plan, version 2 (generic)
## Built on validated instruments, with an adaptive layer for specifics

This is a compatibility and planning instrument for two partners, extensible to co-caregivers and children. It starts from instruments that have been tested on thousands of couples, so the baseline numbers mean something and can be read against published norms. Then it adds an adaptive layer that gets specific about preferences and plans, but only where the baseline shows something worth discussing.

Three rules hold throughout. Nobody is scored as a person; only items, gaps, and domains are scored. Both partners answer everything, about themselves and about each other. Low importance is a real and useful answer.

Notation: the two partners are A and B. A child is C. A third caregiver is G. Replace with real names when you run it.

---

## Layer 0. Individual baselines (about 20 minutes each)

Each partner takes these alone. They describe how each person is wired and let the interpretation read the relationship answers in context. Use the official item text from each source; paraphrased items lose the validation.

- **Mini-IPIP** (Donnellan et al., 2006), 20 items, public domain. Big Five.
- **ECR-R** (Fraley, Waller & Brennan, 2000), 36 items, free. Attachment anxiety and avoidance. The single most useful individual score for a couple.
- **PHQ-9** and **GAD-7** (public domain). Current depression and anxiety. Read as context and never as a diagnosis.
- **SIS/SES-SF** (Carpenter, Janssen et al., 2008). Sexual excitation and two kinds of inhibition.
- **SDI-2** (Spector, Carey & Steinberg, 1996). Dyadic and solitary desire, scored separately.
- Optional, sensitive, self-knowledge only: **OCI-R** (Foa et al., 2002), obsessive-compulsive symptoms.

## Layer 1. The relationship (about 35 minutes each)

Taken alone. Validated instruments, several containing the perception-gap design.

- **CSI-16** (Funk & Rogge, 2007), 16 items, free. Global satisfaction; below 51.5 indicates distress.
- **RDAS** (Busby et al., 1995), 14 items. Consensus, satisfaction, cohesion; 48+ is non-distressed.
- **CPQ-SF** (Futris et al., 2010), 11 items. Demand-withdraw pattern in both directions.
- **ACQ, Areas of Change Questionnaire** (Weiss, Hops & Patterson, 1973), 34 behaviors. How much I want you to change each, and how much I think you want me to change each. Yields desired change and perceptual accuracy.
- **FAPBI** (Doss & Christensen, 2006). Partner behaviors rated for frequency and for acceptability of that frequency.
- **Who Does What?** (Cowan & Cowan, 1988). Tasks, decisions, and childcare, each rated "how it is now" and "how I'd like it," by both partners.
- **Brief CRS, Coparenting Relationship Scale** (Feinberg, Brown & Kan, 2012), 14 items. Coparenting agreement, closeness, support versus undermining, exposure of the child to conflict, endorsement of the partner's parenting, division of labor. The validated home for the parenting axis.
- **MAP, Marital Agendas Protocol** (Notarius & Vanzetti, 1983). Problem intensity in ten areas, then confidence you can resolve each. High intensity plus low confidence is a sticking point; high intensity plus high confidence is a project.
- Optional: **PRQC** (Fletcher, Simpson & Thomas, 2000), 18 items. Satisfaction, commitment, intimacy, trust, passion, love, scored separately.

### The parenting baseline, expanded

The Brief CRS measures how well two caregivers work together. It does not measure what each believes about raising children, which is where values conflict lives. Add one validated parenting-style instrument so the baseline captures the actual dimension of disagreement.

- **PSDQ, Parenting Styles and Dimensions Questionnaire, short form** (Robinson et al., 2001), 32 items, widely used and free for research. Three scores per parent: authoritative (warm and firm, reasons with the child, sets limits with explanation), authoritarian (demands obedience, hierarchical, punitive, "because I said so"), and permissive (few demands, avoids confrontation, inconsistent limits). Each partner scores themselves. The three-way profile is the single clearest read on parenting-values fit, and it maps directly onto the two failure modes couples usually split into: one partner high on authoritarian, the other high on permissive.

Read the PSDQ two ways. Within each parent, which style is highest tells you their default. Between parents, a large gap on the authoritarian or permissive scale is the values conflict, and it predicts exactly the fights where one adult thinks the other is too harsh and the other thinks the first is too soft.

## How the interpretation reads the baseline

The interpreter (a person or an AI) receives both partners' Layer 0 and Layer 1 results and produces one page per domain, stating each reading rule in plain language.

1. State measures first. If either PHQ-9 or GAD-7 is 10 or above for either partner, all relationship scores are read as "under distress," said once.
2. Attachment context. High avoidance reframes low acceptability of closeness behaviors and high withdrawal as a wiring pattern, not a verdict. High anxiety reframes demand scores similarly.
3. Satisfaction and adjustment against cutoffs, plus which RDAS subscale is lowest: consensus points at the domains, cohesion at shared life, satisfaction at conflict.
4. The pattern. CPQ-SF demand-withdraw in both directions, with the ACQ items where the demanding partner wants change.
5. Parenting. The PSDQ profiles side by side, and the Brief CRS subscales. A large between-parent gap on authoritarian or permissive is flagged as a values conflict, not a logistics one.
6. Flags for the color layer, if any of these is true: MAP high intensity and low confidence; ACQ desired change at +2 or beyond, or a perceptual miss of 2 or more; FAPBI "unacceptable" at current frequency by either partner; Who Does What now-versus-ideal gap of 3 or more, or partners' "now" ratings differing by 3 or more; RDAS consensus item at "frequently disagree" or worse; Brief CRS undermining or exposure-to-conflict above midpoint; PSDQ between-parent gap of about one scale point or more on authoritarian or permissive.
7. Aligned items reported by name.
8. No PHQ-9, GAD-7, or OCI-R number shared with the other partner without consent, and no characterization of a person from any score.

Output: three lists per domain, aligned, low-intensity misaligned, and flagged. Only flagged domains open the color layer.

## Layer 2. Add more color (adaptive; only where flagged)

The validated instruments say where the problem is. The color layer says what each partner actually wants instead and what a plan would look like. Run asynchronously, each partner alone, before comparison.

For each flagged domain: specifics (short written scenarios), preference (concrete, with an example the interpreter can request), requirement-or-preference tag (does the relationship work without it), and consistency (checked against the baseline and the person's other answers, at most three gentle probes per domain, each asked once, to that person only, dropped and noted if declined).

### The parenting color module, in depth

This module opens whenever the PSDQ gap, the Brief CRS, or the parenting rows of Who Does What flag. It's the longest module on purpose, because parenting values are the hardest to surface and the easiest to fight about, and because "respect," "structure," and "discipline" mean opposite things to different people.

The module separates parenting into dimensions that people usually blend, and asks each partner about each dimension by itself. Two parents can share a word and disagree on every dimension under it.

**Dimension 1. Warmth and control are independent, not opposite.** The whole point of the research is that a parent can be high on both (authoritative), high on control and low on warmth (authoritarian), high on warmth and low on control (permissive), or low on both. Ask each separately.
- On a normal day, how do you show a child warmth? Give a concrete example from this week if there was one.
- On a normal day, how do you enforce a limit? Give a concrete example.
- Preference: describe a moment where a child was corrected well. What made it good?

**Dimension 2. What "respect" means.** This word hides the biggest conflicts. To one parent it means the child obeys and doesn't talk back to adults. To another it means adults and children treat each other with basic dignity, which includes the adult respecting the child.
- Finish this sentence: "A respectful child is one who ____."
- Finish this sentence: "A respectful parent is one who ____."
- A child disagrees with an adult, out loud, and turns out to be right. What should happen?
- A child asks a blunt, curious question about an adult's body, clothes, or choices, in front of others. Is that disrespect, curiosity, or something else? What do you do?
- Requirement or preference: on whether children may question adults; on what a child owes an adult versus what an adult owes a child.

**Dimension 3. Self-expression versus structure.** This is the axis where an easygoing parent and a structured parent split. Neither pole is correct, and each has a failure mode: too little structure leaves a child adrift, too much leaves a child unable to self-author.
- How much should a child's day be planned versus open? Give your ideal weekday.
- When a child wants to do something unusual (in clothing, interest, behavior) that isn't harmful, what's your response?
- How involved should a parent be in a child's friendships, schoolwork, and activities? Where's the line between supporting and controlling?
- Preference: describe the balance of structure and freedom you'd want for a child at this age.
- Requirement or preference: on daily structure; on how much a child directs their own interests.

**Dimension 4. Consistency between what's modeled and what's required.** Children learn from what adults do, not what they say. A common conflict is one adult holding the child to a standard the adults don't keep (language, screen use, emotional control).
- Name something you expect from a child that you also hold yourself to.
- Name something adults in the house do that you would not want the child to copy.
- Is it fine for adults to use language or habits around a child that the child isn't allowed to use? Where's the line?
- Requirement or preference: on adults modeling what they require.

**Dimension 5. Discipline mechanics.** The actual moves, so "discipline" stops being an abstraction.
- When a limit is broken, what happens, step by step?
- What's off the table entirely as a response to a child?
- Who handles correction when both adults are present, and what does the other do?
- When adults disagree about a child in the moment, what happens in front of the child, and what happens after?
- Requirement or preference: on specific methods; on disagreeing in front of the child; on who has final say.

**Dimension 6. Roles in a blended or multi-caregiver home.** Only if relevant.
- What's each adult's role with the child, and how did you decide it?
- When does a non-primary caregiver correct the child, and when do they hand it to the primary parent?
- What does backing each other up mean, and what does it not require?
- Requirement or preference: on who disciplines; on how a newer caregiver earns standing.

The interpreter's consistency job is heaviest here. Common contradictions to probe, gently and to one person only: a high self-rated warmth score with scenario answers that are all control; a stated belief in respect-as-obedience alongside modeling the interpreter can see the person wouldn't accept from the child; an easygoing self-description with scenario answers showing no structure the person can name. The probe is a question, never a correction: "You rated yourself high on reasoning-with rather than commanding, and your answers here are mostly about obedience. Help me see how those fit."

### Other color modules

Intimacy and affection; communication and nonverbal; conflict and repair; household; self-care and health; friends, family, and the long term. Each runs the same four steps with two or three scenarios apiece. (Same as the prior version; the parenting module above is the expanded one.)

## Layer 3. From color to plan

For each flagged domain, once both partners finish, the interpreter produces a one-page brief: what each said they'd do and why, the values underneath, the requirement-versus-preference tags side by side, and the consistency notes each agreed to share. Written answers summarized unless marked shareable.

Then the couple writes the plan together, one line per item: the topic, what we agreed, what each of us does, when we look at it again. Shared requirements at the top, unrevisited. Requirement-versus-preference mismatches get a date. Parked items noted as parked on purpose.

For parenting specifically, the plan should name: the household's position on children questioning adults; who corrects and how; the structure-versus-freedom balance for this child at this age; the language and modeling standard adults hold; and the protocol for adults disagreeing about the child. Those five lines resolve most of the recurring fights.

Retake the baseline yearly. The validated scores are the only way to tell whether anything moved.

## The interpreter prompt

```
You are reading validated relationship and parenting assessment results for two partners, and running an adaptive follow-up with each partner separately.

Reading rules:
- Report scores against published cutoffs in one plain sentence each. Never diagnose or characterize a person.
- Read all relationship scores as "under distress" if either partner's PHQ-9 or GAD-7 is 10 or above; say so once.
- Use attachment, sexual-response, and parenting-style scores as context that explains patterns, never as verdicts.
- For parenting, report each partner's authoritative/authoritarian/permissive profile and flag a large between-parent gap as a values conflict, not a logistics one.
- Flag domains by the document's rules. Report aligned items by name.
- Never share a PHQ-9, GAD-7, or OCI-R number with the other partner without consent.

Color rules:
- Run color modules only for flagged domains, each partner alone.
- For each: specifics, preference, requirement-or-preference tag, then consistency. No more than three consistency probes per domain, each asked once, gently, to that person only; if declined, drop and note.
- The parenting module is the deep one: ask about warmth and control separately, surface what "respect" and "structure" mean to each person, and check self-ratings against scenario answers.
- Ask for a concrete example whenever an answer is abstract.
- Never take a side. If an answer seems unreasonable, ask a question.
- Summarize written answers in your own words unless marked shareable.
- Frame every agenda item as a joint decision. Plain language, short outputs, no clinical terms.
```

## Extended caregivers and children

**A third caregiver (G).** The Brief CRS works with wording changes, and Who Does What can add a column for that person's tasks. Color questions: what help they want, what help they can give, what role they'd like with the child, what they'd prefer the household not do. If they take real caregiving time, the PSDQ is worth having from them too, because a third adult with a very different parenting profile is a third position to reconcile.

**A child (C).** No validated instrument fits a young child's view of the family, and none should be used. Use a kitchen-table conversation instead: what makes home feel safe and good; how they like to be told when an adult is upset; what they'd like to do with each adult one-on-one; what a fair rule looks like and an unfair one; what a grown-up should do when the child says something the grown-up doesn't like; what the child should do when they have a question about something an adult does. The adults answer the last two as well, so the household rule about questions and disagreements is written partly in the child's words.
